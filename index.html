Original Craftsman homes in Rucker Hill, Everett

Rucker Hill is one of Everett's best neighborhoods for studying early 20th-century architecture. The historic district contains more than 100 older homes, including Craftsman bungalows, larger Craftsman houses, American Foursquares, and Colonial Revival homes. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1989.

Here are three documented examples of original Craftsman homes to look for, along with one architectural distinction worth keeping in mind.

1. McAdams House — 603 33rd Street (1911)

This is identified in the historic district documentation as one of Rucker Hill's finest Craftsman homes. Look for the prominent gables, extended roof eaves, oversized decorative brackets, and strong wood detailing.

2. Morgan House — 727 33rd Street (1915)

The historic documentation identifies this as the W.C. Morgan House. It is a particularly useful example of the smaller Craftsman bungalow form, with period details such as decorative woodwork and natural materials. Property descriptions also mention leaded and stained glass, hardwood floors, and a stone fireplace.

3. Craftsman bungalow — 611 Warren Street (1918)

This home is another example specifically identified in the historic district documentation. The district's bungalows are noted for low-pitched gabled roofs, broad overhanging eaves, exposed rafter tails, decorative brackets, and welcoming front porches.

How to recognize an original Craftsman

When evaluating these homes, distinguish between a historic house that was built in the Craftsman style and a newer house designed to imitate it.

Look for:

  • Roof: Low-pitched gables with wide, overhanging eaves.
  • Structure: Exposed rafter tails and decorative knee braces or brackets.
  • Porch: A deep covered front porch, often with substantial tapered columns.
  • Materials: Original wood shingles, clapboard, stone, or other natural materials.
  • Interior: Fir floors, built-in cabinetry, substantial wood trim, and a fireplace with built-in bookcases.

These are characteristic features, but not every authentic Craftsman has all of them.

For further research, the best starting point is Historic Everett's Rucker Hill historic district resources, which provide documented homes and historical photographs. One important caveat: original construction does not guarantee that every exterior or interior feature remains original today.
